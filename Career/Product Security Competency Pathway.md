# Product Security Career Pathway: Competencies by Level

I built this table from years of actually hiring, promoting, and leveling people on ProdSec teams, not from an HR competency framework someone bought off a shelf. Use it to figure out where you sit today and what the next level actually expects from you, not just what title shows up on the badge.

## Career Level Comparison Table

| Dimension | Associate/Junior | Mid-Level | Senior | Staff/Principal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem Solving & Initiative | Handles well-defined, narrow tasks. Troubleshoots the simple stuff and debugs small chunks of code. Remediation guidance is usually incomplete or surface-level. Limited on operational work. | Spots security issues on their own and proposes full solutions, not patches. Handles moderate complexity independently across a wider scope. | Prevents the issue before it happens instead of reacting to it. Handles complex, multi-part problems solo. Comes up with solutions that shrink the attack surface without breaking the customer's day. | Solves problems before they become company-wide problems. Moves between the hardest, most ambiguous issues regardless of team lines, and the fixes they build get reused by other teams instead of staying local. |
| Strategic Thinking | Task-oriented and can't see why the work matters. Curious, but jumps into execution before understanding the context. | Starting to connect the dots between individual tasks and the bigger project goal. Takes time to understand the why before starting. | Asks why before touching the how, macro before micro. Ties security work directly to business outcomes and thinks through purpose before acting. | Sets technical direction for ProdSec across multiple teams, not just their own. Thinks in multi-quarter horizons and ties architecture choices to where the business is headed, not just where it is today. |
| Autonomy & Guidance | Can't carry a large project alone. Needs a step-by-step plan and regular check-ins. Sticks to established process and asks for approval before any big call. | Works independently with periodic check-ins. Builds project plans with some help. Escalates at the right time instead of too early or too late. | Runs projects start to finish with almost no hand-holding. Builds solid plans with clear communication built in. Only escalates when genuinely blocked by something outside their control. | Runs with full autonomy across the org, project plans ship without needing a review step first. Other senior engineers check their own plans against this person's instead of the other way around. |
| Technical Depth | Still learning the fundamentals and the tools. Applies known techniques to standard, textbook scenarios. On the receiving end of training and workshops. | Solid grip on security principles and can adapt techniques to scenarios that don't match the textbook. Starting to specialize in one or two areas. | Deep technical range across multiple domains. Actively tracks new techniques and trends without being told to. Runs the workshops instead of attending them. | Deep enough in one or two areas to be the org's go-to expert, broad enough everywhere else to hold their own in any ProdSec conversation. Builds the standard instead of just following it. |
| Communication & Influence | Talks mostly within their immediate team, sharing updates on small stuff like a false positive ticket. Still learning how to explain a security concept in plain language. | Explains issues clearly to technical teams and is actively building relationships across functions. Starting to influence team-level calls and talk to non-technical folks. | Breaks down complex technical issues for execs and clients without losing the point. Shapes how the broader org thinks about security, not just how one team handles it. | Influences decisions in rooms they're not even in, because the standard or doc they wrote already answered the question. Briefs the CISO or a VP directly and gets heard without a manager translating it first. |
| Risk Management | Understands compliance requirements exist but can't yet turn them into action items for the team. Needs real support to spot risk. Follows the existing framework as written. | Assesses risk with growing independence, and can translate compliance language into steps someone can actually do. Recommends fixes that fit the business, not just the checklist. | Balances security and business tradeoffs on instinct built from experience. Makes the call independently and owns it. Turns dense compliance requirements into clear team objectives. | Sets the risk appetite and tradeoff framework other teams use to make their own calls, instead of making every call personally. |
| Ownership & Accountability | Owns their individual tasks and finishes them with oversight. Feels pressure to show value fast instead of taking the time to do it right. Needs sign-off before changing anything. | Owns small to medium projects and the deliverables that come with them. Balances speed against quality and loops in the right stakeholders before deciding. | Owns major initiatives end to end and makes sure the team actually follows through, not just ships the plan. Picks doing it right over doing it fast every time. | Owns outcomes across multiple teams or a whole program area, secrets, detection, SDLC tooling, and stays accountable even when they didn't write the code or run the incident themselves. |
| Mentorship & Leadership | On the receiving end of mentorship, seeking out learning and showing up to workshops. | Starting to mentor junior folks and guide them through familiar territory. Runs small working groups. | Actively develops junior and mid-level engineers, runs the workshops, and sets the technical standard the rest of the team works against. | Multiplies the whole team's output by building the tools, docs, and standards that make every other engineer faster, not just mentoring one person at a time. |
| Scope of Impact | Individual contributor, impact limited to their own task. | Project-level impact, starting to shape team practice and tooling choices. | Org-wide impact, shaping strategy, standards, and culture across multiple teams. | Org-wide, sometimes multi-org impact. Shapes how security scales as the company grows, not just how one team practices it today. |
| Decision Making | Follows the existing playbook and needs approval for anything beyond routine. Checks in frequently before acting. | Makes confident calls inside their project's scope, pulling in the right stakeholders without needing approval for routine decisions. | Makes high-stakes security and architecture calls independently, backed by real experience. Trusted advisor to leadership, no approval chain required. | Makes calls that set precedent for how the whole org handles a class of problem, and other senior engineers cite that decision when making their own. |
| Incident Response | Supports response under guidance, documents findings, follows the runbook. | Leads response on moderate incidents, runs the analysis, and writes the remediation plan. | Leads critical incident response, coordinates across teams under pressure, runs the postmortem, and drives the fix that stops it from happening again. | Gets pulled into the incident everyone else is stuck on, not because of title but because of track record. Fixes the systemic gap afterward, not just the fire in front of them. |
| Process & Tooling | Uses what's already there and suggests tweaks based on what they've noticed. | Improves and tunes existing process, evaluates new tools and makes the case for them. | Designs the process and the framework from scratch, architects the tooling strategy, and pushes automation so the program grows without adding headcount. | Builds the shared platform, detection pipelines, secrets management, scanning in CI/CD, that the rest of ProdSec runs on, and sets the bar everyone else gets measured against. |
| Collaboration | Works well inside their own team, still building connections elsewhere. | Collaborates well across teams, partners directly with engineering, product, and infra. | Builds strategic partnerships across the org, shapes product roadmaps and engineering practice, represents security in the room with execs. | Trusted across the exec team and treated as a peer by senior engineering leadership, not just the security person in the room. |
| Meeting Participation & Representation | Either skips the critical meetings or sits in without saying much. | Attends the important meetings and actually contributes, starting to represent the team in cross-functional settings. | Stands in for the program owner's goals and outlook, covers critical meetings, and makes calls on leadership's behalf when needed. | Represents ProdSec in rooms the Director or CISO can't be in every time, and their read on a decision carries the same weight as if the Director had made it themselves. |

## Where the Ladder Splits: IC Track vs Management Track

Past Senior, the path forks. One side keeps going deep technical, Staff, then Principal, then Distinguished if your org goes that far, still an individual contributor but with org-wide authority over architecture and standards. The other side goes people and org, Security Manager, then Director, then eventually CISO.

Both are real careers. The thing to watch for, and the thing I push back on when I see it, is a Staff or Principal title that doesn't come with real authority, no say in architecture decisions, no budget influence, no seat at the table a Director gets by default. That's not a track, that's a consolation prize for someone who didn't want to manage people, and your best engineers will leave the second they figure that out.

### What Staff+ ProdSec Actually Looks Like

If you're wondering what the IC side of that fork actually does day to day, it tends to land in one of four patterns:

- Tech Lead, owns the security posture and technical approach for one specific product area or team, the person everyone in that domain goes to first.
- Architect, sets secure design and architecture standards across multiple teams or the whole org, and stays in that lane for the long haul.
- Solver, gets dropped into the gnarliest problems, a bad breach, a migration everyone's been avoiding, a vendor contract that's clearly wrong, and moves on once it's fixed.
- Right Hand, extends the Director or CISO's reach, picks up whatever's highest priority that week, trusted enough to make the call on their behalf.

Most staff+ people are some mix of these, not a pure version of one. Knowing which one fits you tells you what to ask for next instead of just chasing the next title.

## Beyond the Table: What Else Senior Folks Bring

The table covers the core competencies, but every senior ProdSec person I've hired or promoted also brings a few things that don't fit neatly into a column.

- Thought leadership, they show up at conferences, write the blog post, and send the pull request to the open source security project instead of just filing an issue.
- Business acumen, they know the revenue number and prioritize against it, and they can brief a VP without translating everything into security jargon first.
- Crisis management, they stay calm when the room is panicking at 2am and make the call anyway.
- Continuous improvement, they push the maturity initiatives before anyone asks for them and build the metrics that prove the program works, not just that it's busy.
- Vendor management, they run the bake-offs and hold vendors to what the sales deck promised.
- Compliance expertise, they take SOC2 or ISO or GDPR and turn it into three sentences an engineer can actually act on.
- Adaptability, when the threats shift or the business pivots or the org chart gets redrawn again, they adjust the plan instead of defending the old one.

If you're not sure where you land, pull up this table in your next 1:1 and ask your manager to grade you against it. Then start logging the moments you actually hit the bar for the next column, write it down the week it happens, not the week before your review, because that's the paper trail that turns "I think I'm ready" into a promotion case.
